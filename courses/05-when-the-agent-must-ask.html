Part 1 · covers the lethal trifecta

Three legs, and why you cut one

5 min live

Three capabilities are each harmless on their own and dangerous together: reading private data, reading text a stranger wrote, and being able to send something out. Halyard's agent has all three on its first afternoon, not through any one bad decision but because each tool was reasonable when it was added. Draw the legs with the real tools on them and the attack path draws itself.

LiveWillison's three legs2 min▶

The three parts. Simon Willison's post of 16 June 2025 (read at source) names them: access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally. An agent with all three can be tricked by an attacker into exfiltrating data: the untrusted content carries the instruction, the private data is the payload, and the external channel is the exit.

Why detection is not the answer. Guardrail products that cite roughly 95% detection are, in his words about web security, a failing grade: an attacker gets to try again, and a 1-in-20 miss rate is a working exploit. His advice is structural, to avoid the combination entirely rather than filter it.

Why it arrives by accident. The post points at MCP's mix-and-match model: install one server that reads your email and another that fetches URLs, and you have assembled all three legs without designing anything. Halyard did exactly that, one reasonable tool at a time.

LiveWhich leg Halyard cuts, and why that one2 min▶

Not untrusted input. The agent exists to read tickets that customers write. Stop it reading them and there is no agent.

Not private data. Reading the support inbox, the channels and the charges is the job too. Sessions 3 and 4 already cut this leg as far as it goes: the tickets tool reads with gmail.readonly, nothing broader.

The way out. What is left is the exit: sending email to any address and fetching any URL. Neither needs to be removed. Both need a person in front of them when the destination is not already trusted. That is the gate in the figure, and it is the bench's trifecta flag: it reads present on rungs 0 to 3 and broken at rung 4, the rung where send email and fetch sit behind approval.

LiveOWASP LLM06: excessive autonomy, and "require user approval"1 min▶

OWASP's LLM06:2025 Excessive Agency (read at source) has three root causes: excessive functionality, excessive permissions and excessive autonomy. Sessions 3 and 4 dealt with the first two. Excessive autonomy is the third: high-impact actions taken without anyone independently verifying or approving them, such as a delete with no confirmation.

Its prevention list includes "require user approval" for high-impact actions. Its own example scenario is close to Halyard's: an email-summary assistant whose plugin can also send, where an indirect prompt injection forwards inbox data. The fix it gives is a read-only extension, a read-only OAuth scope, and the user approving each send.

Real world

A name that stuck because it is checkable. Willison's "lethal trifecta" post turned a vague worry into a three-item checklist you can run against any agent design in a minute: list the tools, mark each one private data, untrusted input or way out, and see if all three columns are filled. His closing advice fits in a line: "avoid that lethal trifecta combination entirely."

Part 2 · covers approval gates for irreversible and high-value actions

Gates where a mistake cannot be undone

5 min live

A gate is a pause between the agent choosing a call and the call running. A person looks, then says yes or no, and the decision is written down. The design work is in three choices: which calls wait, what the person is shown, and what happens when nobody answers. Get the second one wrong and the gate approves whatever the model says it is doing.

LiveWhat to gate, and what not to2 min▶

Gate a call when any one of three things is true. It cannot be undone (an email, once sent, is read). It moves value (a refund, however capped). It crosses a trust boundary (a fetch to a host nobody has vetted carries whatever is in its query string out of Halyard).

Do not gate reads. Reading the inbox, the channels and the charges is the agent's job, happens all day, and changes nothing. A gate on reads buys a stream of prompts and no safety the scope table did not already give you.

Do not gate your own channel. A post to the ops team's own Slack channel with chat:write reaches people who already work there. Session 3 removed chat:write.public, so the agent cannot post anywhere else.

LiveShow the arguments, not the summary1 min▶

A poisoned ticket that can steer the call can also steer the model's description of the call. If the approval prompt says "replying to the customer", that sentence was written by the same model the attacker is talking to. The gate must render the actual arguments the tool will receive: the full recipient list, the attachment names, the refund amount and charge id, the full URL including its query string. The approver checks the call, not the story.

LiveAllowlists: the lane that does not wait1 min▶

Most of Halyard's fetches go to the same few carrier tracking sites. Gating every one of them would teach the approver to click yes. Instead, a named allowlist of carrier tracking domains is an auto-approve lane: a fetch to a domain on the list runs and is logged; a fetch anywhere else waits for a person. The allowlist is itself a control, so it has an owner and a review date, and adding a domain is a change someone signs.

The same lane works for email: replies to the address that opened the ticket could go straight through. Halyard's constructed design does not take that lane yet, and session 6's audit record is what would let you decide later.

Self-studyGates are the last line, not the only one2 min▶

OWASP LLM06 is careful here: logging, monitoring and rate limiting limit the damage of excessive agency but do not prevent it. A gate is the same kind of control. It catches what reaches it; it does nothing about what the scope table should have removed. That is why gates come fifth in Halyard's plan, after scopes and the broker, and why session 5's bench work starts on rung 3 rather than rung 0.

A gate is also a different control from a guardrail. A guardrail inspects the model's output and blocks text that looks wrong; how to build those is taught in learn-ai-agents, guardrails. A gate asks a person about a specific call with specific arguments, whatever the text around it looked like.

Decide the timeout before the first prompt fires. A gate with no timeout either blocks the queue forever or, worse, gets a quiet "auto-approve if nobody answers" patch in the middle of the night. Write down that silence means reject, and who gets paged instead.

Part 3 · covers injection-resistant designs and approval fatigue, taught as a disagreement

Designs against fatigue: the open argument

5 min live

There are two serious answers to "the agent read something poisoned". One says: shape the agent so that untrusted text cannot change what it does next, and you need fewer gates. The other says: shapes cost so much capability that teams ship gates instead, and the real risk is a person who approves whatever they are shown. Both sides have a point. Neither has the measurement that would end the argument.

LiveSix design patterns, and the principle under them2 min▶

Beurer-Kellner and colleagues, "Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents against Prompt Injections" (2025, arXiv 2506.08837, read at source) start from one principle: once an agent has ingested untrusted input, it must be constrained so that input cannot trigger consequential actions. They give six patterns, each a different way to enforce that.

PatternThe constraintWhere it would sit at Halyard
Action-Selectorthe model maps a request to a predefined tool call, with no feedback from results"look up order status" buttons for reps
Plan-Then-Executea fixed plan is committed before untrusted data is readplan "read ticket, look up charge, draft reply" first
LLM Map-Reduceisolated sub-agents each handle one untrusted item; a separate step combinessummarising a day of tickets one by one
Dual LLMa privileged model holds the tools; a quarantined model with no tools reads untrusted textthe quarantined model reads the ticket body
Code-Then-Executethe agent writes a program that calls tools and sub-models, then it runsa refund workflow written once, run per ticket
Context-Minimizationthe user's prompt is removed from context after it has guided the first actiondrop the ticket text before the reply is sent

The Halyard column is constructed. The paper's own point is that every pattern costs some utility: an agent constrained this way cannot do some tasks a free agent could, and the case studies discuss that trade directly.

LiveCaMeL: security by construction, and its price1 min▶

Debenedetti and colleagues, "Defeating Prompt Injections by Design" (CaMeL, 2025, arXiv 2503.18813; the abstract was read at source) put a protective system layer around the model. It extracts the control flow and data flow from the trusted user query, so data retrieved later cannot change which steps run, and it uses capabilities to enforce security policies at each tool call, which stops private data leaving through a flow nobody authorised. It builds on the Dual LLM pattern.

The abstract reports the price on the AgentDojo benchmark: 77% of tasks solved with provable security, against 84% for an undefended system. Read that both ways. Seven points of tasks is the cost of a guarantee; a guarantee is what a 95% detector cannot give you.

LiveThe counter-argument: approval fatigue2 min▶

The claim. People approve what they are shown, if they are shown enough of it. A gate that fires all shift stops being read; the latest "Approve send_email?" gets the same click as the first one did. Too many gates become no gates, and they also teach the team that the agent is supervised when it is not.

The evidence. This is a practitioner argument. No primary measurement of approval fatigue for agent gates was found in the sources behind this page, so treat it as a strong intuition, not a result.

The disagreement, stated fairly. Design patterns cost utility and engineering time, and most teams have neither to spare this quarter. Gates cost attention, which is cheap on day one and scarce by month three. The pattern side can point to a measured cost (CaMeL's 77 against 84) and a guarantee; the gate side can point to the fact that gates ship this week. Nobody has measured both on the same agent. Halyard's constructed design takes the gate path for now, keeps the gated list short on purpose, and treats a pattern like Plan-Then-Execute as the next move if the approval log shows people clicking through.

Self-studyOWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications2 min▶

OWASP released a Top 10 for Agentic Applications on December 9, 2025 (release date read at its landing page). The list itself sits in a downloadable document that was not read for this page; the item names are known only from secondary sources, so they are reported, not reproduced here. Read it at source and map each item to your gate rules.

Whether a given design actually resists injection is a testing question, and it is taught in learn-ai-red-team, prompt injection and learn-ai-red-team, tool abuse and agency. Those sessions attack the design built here; this one only builds it.

Real world

A measured price for a guarantee. CaMeL's abstract is unusual because it prints the cost next to the protection: on AgentDojo, provable security came with 77% of tasks solved, against 84% undefended. That is the kind of number the approval-fatigue side does not yet have, which is why the argument is still open.

Build-along

Place Halyard's gates

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The bench below starts on rung 3: one scoped key per tool, all behind the broker, no gates. Move the Approval gates control and watch which actions drop out of reach, which stay, and what happens to the trifecta flag. Then try the shortcut every team is tempted by, gates on the day-one key, and read why it is not enough. The weights are constructed for teaching, so read the weighted number as a comparison between rungs, not a price.

Read rung 3 before you touch anything. The bench prints 6 of 19 sensitive actions reachable, a weighted blast radius of 26 (constructed weights), and the trifecta flag present. The shortest injected path is ticket body, ops agent, tickets tool, send email to any address. No key leaks; the agent's own access is enough.

★ Step 1 · starting point, rung 3rung 3 (broker on, no gates): 6 of 19 reachable, weighted 26 trifecta: present path: ticket body -> agent -> tickets tool -> send email to any address reachable: read the support inbox, send email to any address, read every public channel, read charges, refund up to 200 USD, fetch any URL

Set Approval gates to "Irreversible actions". The bench prints 3 of 19, weighted 11, 3 approvals, and the trifecta flag flips to broken. The three gated actions are send email, refund up to 200 USD and fetch any URL. What is left is three reads: read the support inbox, read every public channel, read charges. The private-data leg is still there; the way out is not.

★ Step 2 · gates on irreversible actionsgates: irreversible actions -> 3 of 19, weighted 11, 3 approvals trifecta: broken gated: send email, refund up to 200 USD, fetch any URL still reachable (reads): support inbox, every public channel, charges

Set gates to "Every write" and compare. Look at the reachable list. It is the same three reads. Gating more writes moves nothing here, because nothing reachable is a write any more. More gates past this point only add prompts, which is the fatigue argument from Part 3 showing up on the bench. Set gates back to "Irreversible actions".

★ Step 3 · every write, same three readsgates: every write -> reachable list unchanged: read the support inbox, read every public channel, read charges lesson: once only reads are left, more gates buy prompts, not safety decision: keep gates on irreversible actions

Spring the trap: gates on the day-one key. Click ladder 0 (day one), then set Approval gates to "Irreversible actions". The bench prints 6 of 19, weighted 24, with 9 approvals, and the trifecta reads broken. Six reads and posts are still reachable: read the support inbox, read every public channel, post in any channel, read staff email addresses, read the customer contact table, read charges. Gates on a broad key ask a person nine times and still leave as many sensitive actions open as rung 3 did with no gates at all.

★ Step 4 · the trap, gates on the shared keyrung 0 + gates on irreversible actions: 6 of 19, weighted 24, 9 approvals, trifecta broken still reachable: read the support inbox, read every public channel, post in any channel, read staff email addresses, read the customer contact table, read charges lesson: gates do not replace scopes; they sit on top of them

Write the gate rules. Five columns per gated action: what it is, who approves, what the approver sees, how long before silence means no, and what skips the gate. The values below are constructed for Halyard; change the people and timeouts to fit your own on-call.

★ Step 5 · gate rules (constructed)action: send email approver: support lead (Dana) or on-call sees: every recipient, subject, full body, attachment names timeout: 15 min, then reject and page on-call skips gate: nothing yet action: refund up to 200 USD approver: the requesting rep (refunds run on their behalf) sees: charge id, amount, customer, reason text from the ticket timeout: 15 min, then reject action: fetch approver: on-call sees: the full URL, including the query string timeout: 5 min, then reject skips gate: carrier tracking domains on the named allowlist (owner: platform team, reviewed monthly) reads (inbox, channels, charges): no gate, logged

File it as page 5 of the access plan. It matches Halyard's agreed design: send email, refund, and fetch outside an allowlist of carrier tracking domains need a person's approval; reads run and are logged. Every decision a gate makes is a row session 6 will need.

★ Step 6 · access plan, page 5 of 6: gate rulesGated (a person approves, sees the exact arguments): send email, refund up to 200 USD, fetch outside the carrier allowlist Not gated: reads (inbox, channels, charges), allowlisted fetch Bench: rung 3 -> rung 4 = 6 -> 3 of 19, weighted 26 -> 11 (constructed weights), 3 approvals, trifecta broken Not enough alone: gates on the day-one key = 6 of 19, 9 approvals Open question: revisit a design pattern if approvals get rubber-stamped Feeds session 6: every decision recorded with its approver

Three is a good number of approvals. On rung 4 the gates ask about three kinds of call. On the trap they ask about nine and protect less. When you are tempted to add a gate, first ask whether a scope cut would remove the action instead.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Run the trifecta check on your own agent. List every tool and mark it private data, untrusted input or way out. If all three columns are filled, name the leg you will cut.
  2. Draft one approval prompt. For your riskiest write, write what the approver should see, argument by argument. Then check that none of it comes from the model's own summary.
  3. Write your allowlist. For each outbound tool, list the destinations that could skip the gate, who owns the list, and when it is reviewed.
  4. Pick your side, provisionally. In three sentences, say whether your agent should take a design pattern or gates first, and what evidence would make you switch.
